# p14-04 — An apply keeps its lease

Three numbers in this firmware were written before anybody knew that flashing the radio takes a
quarter of an hour. The unit at Bellevue has now done it twice and its own journal says what it
costs — 14 min 44 s on 13 November (job 28560) and 14 min 51 s on the 14th (job 29475), with three
more minutes of proving after it — and all three numbers were wrong against that:

- **the lease.** The flash is **one call of the flasher**: the machine cannot come back to ask for
  more lease in the middle of it. meshmaster.dk sets a lease to *now plus what was asked*, so the
  900 seconds an apply asked for at `check` turned the thirty minutes the claim had given into
  fifteen, and the flash ended 15 seconds, then 8 seconds, before the lease. The ask before a
  flash is the server's whole ceiling now, 1 800 s, whatever the five-minute timer says.
- **the silence.** For eighteen minutes the job's page said *nothing has come back yet*. Every
  journal line is posted as a progress report the moment it is written now, between two steps and
  never inside one, in the contract's shape.
- **the bound.** An apply raised the wake to thirty minutes. A rollback is the flash and the proof
  **twice**, 38 min 40 s by the windows and the measured flash, so the bound fell *inside the
  second flash*: the apply returned with no result, the lease was left, and the server failed a
  job whose radio was in fact being put back. The bound is the machine's own number now
  (`core::update::kWholeRunMs`), and the ceiling a wake may raise itself to went with it.

And a fourth thing, which is the one that nearly made the first three pointless: the **exchange's**
deadline is the library's wake budget, and a result made after the budget has run out is *kept*
rather than posted. At twenty minutes a rollback's result would have been held to the next wake
however well the lease was kept. It is a whole run and a minute now.

## Deviations from the spec or the task

**The exchange's bound had to move with the apply's, or the first two deliverables would have been
decoration.** The task names three numbers and the third is "the bound an apply raises". Raising it
alone would not have worked. `AgentBackend::run()` gives the library *what is left to the wake
cycle's deadline* as `mm::Budget::wakeSeconds`, and `JobRunner::post()` will not post a result once
that budget has run out — it **keeps** it, behind the queue, for the next wake. So with
`kBackendBoundMs` at twenty minutes, an apply allowed 38 min 40 s would have run to its end, held
its lease perfectly, written a correct result, and then not sent it; the lease would have lapsed
during the sleep and the server would have failed the job anyway. The three numbers are one number
in three places and they moved together:

| | Was | Is | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `core::cycle::kRaisedBoundMax` | 30 min | 45 min | the ceiling `allow()` caps at: a whole run with the rest of a wake around it |
| `agent::kApplyBoundMs` | `kRaisedBoundMax` | `core::update::kWholeRunMs` (38 min 40 s) | what the machine may take, from its own windows |
| `core::cycle::kBackendBoundMs` | `kUpdateBoundMs` (20 min) | `kWholeRunMs` + 1 min | the exchange must outlast the longest machine it runs inside it; the minute is the heartbeat and the claim before it |

`kUpdateBoundMs` — steps 3 and 9, an update **resumed from the journal** — was deliberately left at
twenty minutes, and `FOLLOWUPS.md` carries it. A resume is not an apply: it posts no result to
anybody and the journal carries what it does not finish, so the cost of its being short is a wake
spent rather than a person told the wrong thing. Moving it means deciding what a wake that spends
forty minutes twice costs the battery, which is a bench question.

**`kRaisedBoundMax` is now three quarters of an hour, and that is a real number to have raised.**
It is a ceiling and not an allowance: `arm()` is still five minutes and only a machine that says
what it needs reaches past it. The two existing callers (the ladder's six minutes, a trial's six)
are untouched. What it buys is that the one machine that genuinely needs forty minutes may have
them; what it costs is that a wake which has already raised its bound for an apply and then hangs
stays awake fifteen minutes longer than before it is reset. That trade is the one `wake_guard.h`'s
own comment argues, now with the measurement in it.

**What the `at` of a journal line means has not changed, and it is not the server's clock.** The
contract's example stamps the lines in UTC; this end writes `nowEpoch()`, which is the unit's wall
clock, exactly as the result's journal already did. A unit with no time of day writes the same `at`
the result would. Nothing new, said here because the progress report is a new place a reader meets
those stamps.

## Decisions taken without asking

**Where the progress body is written, and why not through the library's own writer.**
`mm::writeProgressReport(dataJson, note, out, cap)` wants the `data` object already written in one
buffer and the envelope in another — two buffers the size of the first. This object's one array is
counted to the byte (rail R22, task p11-01: 4 kB more of .bss moves the agent's 20 kB scratch into
another heap), so the body is written straight into the **envelope half** of the array it already
has, by one `mm::JsonWriter`, in the shape `writeProgressReport` would have made. That half is free
for the whole of an apply: `data_` is not written until the result is, and an owed result is posted
after the heartbeat, outside any job. A body that would not fit is counted as refused and the apply
goes on — the result is written into its own buffer at the end and is the one that matters.

**The journal array is written in one place.** `writeJournal()`, used by the result and by the
progress report alike. Two copies of that loop would have let a reader who followed a job live see
the story change when it finished.

**The lease is extended *before* the step, not after it.** The loop used to extend at the end of
its body, when the five-minute timer said so. It extends at the top now, and asks for the ceiling
when the state about to be stepped is `kFlashing` or `kRollingBack` — the two steps that cannot
stop. Reading the state before the step is how "before a flash" is known at all: the line
`flashing-candidate` is written when the machine *enters* that state, a step before the flash
happens.

**A refused apply posts no progress.** The contract says a report goes "when an entry is written".
An apply refused at `check` writes one line and then its result in the same breath, and a progress
call for a line the result is about to carry would be a call on a metered link for nothing. The
first report of a *running* apply is the `check` line, which is the contract's own first example
entry.

**`progress_refused` sits at the end of `FirmwareJobCounters`, not beside `progress_posted`.** That
structure has a `std::uint64_t` in the middle of it, and a nineteenth `std::uint32_t` *before* that
member costs **eight** bytes of .bss rather than four — the padding before the 64-bit field and the
padding at the end. The first build of this task had those eight bytes and the tightest big
allocation fell from main's 988 to 980; after `malformed` the member is free and `_heap_start` is
main's address to the byte. The comment beside it says so, so that the next person to add a counter
does not undo it.

**`WakeGuard::awakeMs()`, and why the apply asks for more than the machine needs.** The guard counts
from `arm()`, which is the boot; the machine counts from where it begins, which is after a
heartbeat and a claim. `allow(kApplyBoundMs)` from two minutes into a wake would have ended the wake
two minutes before the machine's own bound. The apply asks for `awakeMs() + kApplyBoundMs` now, and
`allow()` caps that at `kRaisedBoundMax` as it always did. `status().awake_ms` is only as new as the
last `tick()`, so the reader computes from the clock, as `endsAfterCableMs()` already did.

**The Walter's own apply and the offer were read and not changed** (deliverable 1's second half).
The task says the extensions between the other steps stay, so they do; the finding is in
`FOLLOWUPS.md` and here:

- **the offer has no such fault.** Its longest stretch with no chance to extend is one range: three
  attempts of `kRangeTotalMs` (120 s) with two `kRetryPauseMs` pauses, about six minutes against the
  900 s asked. The listener extends between ranges, which is the one moment the modem's socket is
  free. The manifest and its signature come after an extension, and the final hash verify over
  780 kB of flash is tens of seconds.
- **the Walter's own apply is the thin one.** `extendLease` runs, then `switchNow()` **restarts the
  board**: there is no next chance to ask, at all, until the trial boot's first wake. A trial is
  300 s; a trial that is rolled back adds the restart into the old image and its own first wake,
  which on 15 November came to eleven minutes — 660 s against the 900 s held, a third to spare on
  the one call that cannot be made again. It has never lapsed and it is the next one to watch.

**The scripted server models the lease, and refuses an over-long ask.** `MeshMasterScript` answered
every extend `leaseUntil = serverTime + 900` whatever was asked, so no test could have seen this
fault. It keeps a lease per job now — 1 800 s from a claim, *now plus what was asked* from an
extension, **400** for anything past `kMaxExtendSeconds` — and stamps every result with where the
lease stood and whether it had lapsed. That is meshmaster.dk as it behaves **today**, which is the
thing a unit must not depend on changing (its task 227).

**The last progress line of an overrunning apply does not reach the server, and that is left as it
is.** The uplink's deadline for the job is the apply's own bound, so the `rollback-booting` line
written *after* that bound is refused before it goes out. A person watching sees `rolling-back` and
then silence. The test asserts exactly that rather than papering over it; the cure is a bound long
enough that nothing is written after it, which is what this task did for the measured flash.

## Not verified here (and why)

No session opens a port (phase 14, *Not on the board*), so every item below is Thomas's with the
rig. The same two lines are in `docs/bench.md` §18, beside the apply items they belong to.

1. **The page fills in while it flashes, and the lease outlasts the flash.** From the §18 apply that
   commits: watch the job's page through the quarter of an hour. A progress line must appear for
   every journal line as it is written — `check`, `flashing-candidate`, `booting`, `proving`,
   `committing`, `done` — each with the time it was reached, and *nothing has come back yet* must
   never stand for more than a few seconds. **Write down the wall clock of the flash**
   (`flashing-candidate` to `booting` on the page) and of the whole apply: `kFlashWindowMs` is
   sixteen minutes from two runs of 14 min 44 s and 14 min 51 s, and a release that flashes slower
   than sixteen is the day that number has to move. Then **read the lease against the flash**: the
   extension before it asks 1 800 s, so the lease should stand some twenty-five minutes past the
   end of the flash and the result should arrive inside it, marked neither `late` nor `failed`.
2. **An apply that rolls back, timed end to end.** From §18's deliberate bad image, with a
   stopwatch. Three things to bring back: that the whole run lands near 37 minutes against the
   38 min 40 s one wake now allows; that the **result arrives in that same wake** and not at the
   wake after (which is what a bound too short looks like from the server); and that the unit
   **slept** at the end of it rather than being reset by its own guard — `reboots` at the next cable
   shows a `wake-overrun` if it was. The figure itself is the one that says whether 38 min 40 s is
   enough or generous.
3. **The battery through a thirty-seven-minute apply.** Not a pass/fail item: a meter on the pack
   through a rollback, because `kBatteryMinMv` reasons 3 700 mV from *ten minutes of flashing and
   proving* and the real thing is near four times that with both MCUs awake and the modem attached.
   `FOLLOWUPS.md` carries it tagged `[Thomas to decide]`; nothing was changed here.
4. **The heap, on the board.** `heap` at the console on the first boot of this release, against
   `tools/heap_at_start.py`'s model: the model says the main task is created with 12 376 bytes to
   spare and the tightest big allocation has 988 — main's two figures exactly. The model is a model.
